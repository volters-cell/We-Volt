About the data

EU Tracker holds every main roll-call vote the European Parliament has taken since its term began on 16 July 2024: how every member voted, by name, by political group and by member state. The chamber has 720 seats. This page says where that comes from, how complete it is, and what has been done to it.

Where the votes come from

When the Parliament votes by roll call, it records who voted for, who voted against and who abstained, member by member, and publishes that record on its open data portal. That record is the source. Everything here derives from it.

Four things, all published by the Parliament at data.europarl.europa.eu, are all this project reads:

DocumentWhat it gives
A sitting's decisionsEvery member's vote, one by one, for every roll call of that day
The vote items behind themWhat was being voted on: the title, the report, the procedure
The list of membersNames, countries and political groups
The list of sittingsWhen each plenary sits, and whether in Strasbourg or Brussels

An automated job reads them during each sitting and again each night. Whether a text carried is published later than the votes themselves, so a same-day import states the result as derived from the totals, and the later run replaces it with the Parliament's own.

How complete this is

Checked on 24 August 2026, vote by vote, against the Parliament's own record of every sitting of this term:

Sittings since 16 July 2024112
Roll-call votes at those sittings5,891
Of them, votes on a text rather than an amendment679
Held here647
Recorded as roll calls, but with no ballots published32
Votes here whose figures disagree with the Parliament's0

So every vote on a text for which the Parliament publishes individual ballots is here, and every tally matches theirs. The remaining 32 are votes the Parliament lists as roll calls without publishing who voted how; there is nothing to hold. The check is a script in the repository, it runs monthly, and its result is written to data/reference/coverage.json — so this table is a checked number rather than a claim.

Amendment votes — the other 5,212 — are deliberately not imported. A plenary produces hundreds, almost all procedural, and importing them would bury the decisions a reader came for. The importer can take them on request.

What has been done to it

  • Nothing has been interpreted. A member state is coloured by where most of its delegation voted, which is arithmetic. There is no scoring, ranking, or judgement of members, groups or countries anywhere in this project.
  • Only main votes are imported. A plenary produces hundreds of roll calls, mostly on amendments. The archive keeps the vote on each text as a whole; the importer can take everything with one flag.
  • Identities are stored once. Each vote records pairs of member id and position rather than repeating names, which is why a five-year term is about 100 MB of static files rather than 0.9 GB, and why the site costs nothing to run.
  • Every record is checked before it is published. A validator refuses records whose ballots name members the directory does not know, whose group totals do not add up, or whose sources are missing.

How to check it, and how to correct it

Every record is a JSON file in the repository, with its sources inside it. Nothing is edited silently: a correction is a commit, visible in the history. If you find an error, open an issue against the repository.

Political group marks

Each group row carries a tile in the colour that group is conventionally drawn in on a seat chart. Those colours are approximations kept in one place in the code, not official brand values.

A group's own logo replaces the tile as soon as an artwork file is added under assets/groups/. The logos are the groups' trademarks: using a mark to identify the thing it belongs to is normally defensible, but it is not a licence, so take artwork from the group's own press materials, check the terms published there, and never recolour or redraw a mark.
